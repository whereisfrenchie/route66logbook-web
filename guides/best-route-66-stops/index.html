More than a Route 66 checklist

Ask someone what they want to see on Route 66 and a familiar list usually appears: Cadillac Ranch, the Blue Whale of Catoosa, the Gemini Giant, Midpoint Café, the Wigwam Motels, Seligman, Oatman and the Santa Monica Pier. They are famous for a reason. Many are wonderfully strange, ridiculously photogenic and so closely tied to the Mother Road that it would feel wrong to drive past them.

But the more you learn about Route 66, the more interesting the spaces between those icons become.

A filling station you might barely notice can explain how oil companies competed for motorists in the 1930s. An old motel can show how Americans travelled before the Interstate system. A strange piece of abandoned pavement can turn out to be an earlier alignment of the highway, while a theatre, trading post or railroad hotel might have a history stretching back decades before Route 66 even existed.

The road itself was constantly changing. It was rerouted, widened and straightened as cars became faster and traffic increased. Towns that once sat directly on Route 66 suddenly found themselves bypassed, while businesses sometimes moved, rebuilt or changed completely just to keep up with the traffic. By the time the Interstate system arrived, some communities lost in a few years what the old highway had spent decades creating.

That is what makes Route 66 so much more than a collection of roadside attractions. The famous landmarks are a great place to start, but they are only part of the story.

This guide follows some of the best stops from Chicago to California, state by state, not as a definitive checklist but as a way into that history. Some are famous, some are easy to miss, and all of them tell us something about the road and the people who travelled, worked and lived along it.

Illinois

All states ↑

Route 66 begins in Chicago before crossing hundreds of miles of Illinois farmland, small towns and former highway communities. Several alignments survive across the state, which makes Illinois particularly good for understanding that Route 66 was never one fixed road.

Chicago and the beginning of Route 66

When Route 66 was established in 1926, its eastern terminus was in Chicago. The exact route through the city changed over the years, but Chicago remains the traditional starting point for travellers heading west.

Lou Mitchell's is closely associated with that departure. The restaurant opened in 1923, three years before Route 66 was designated, and its location near the historic starting point made it a natural stop for generations of motorists setting out on the road.

Starting here also gives the journey a sense of scale that is easy to lose when Route 66 is presented as a collection of small towns and desert landscapes. You begin in one of America's largest cities and gradually watch the urban landscape give way to suburbs, farmland and the smaller communities that once depended heavily on highway traffic.

Gemini Giant, Wilmington

  • Kitsch

The Gemini Giant is one of the most recognisable examples of the enormous fibreglass figures that appeared outside American roadside businesses from the 1960s onwards. These figures are generally known as Muffler Men, although they advertised everything from restaurants to tyre shops.

Standing around 28 feet tall, the Gemini Giant turned that familiar advertising formula into a space-age mascot, complete with helmet and rocket. His enormous size made perfect sense in an increasingly car-oriented landscape, where a roadside business had only a few seconds to catch the attention of a passing driver.

For most of his life, the Gemini Giant stood outside the Launching Pad Drive-In, where he had welcomed motorists since 1965. After the restaurant closed, the statue was sold at auction in 2024 and acquired locally so that it could remain in Wilmington. He now stands in South Island Park, beginning a new chapter as a preserved piece of roadside history rather than an advertisement for a single business.

Standard Oil Gasoline Station, Odell

  • Gas Station
  • History

Odell's restored filling station is much quieter than the Gemini Giant, but it tells an equally interesting story about the early automobile era.

Built in 1932, the station was designed to resemble a small house rather than an industrial fuel facility. This was not accidental. As filling stations became increasingly common, oil companies discovered that domestic architecture could make them appear cleaner, safer and more trustworthy to motorists.

The business evolved as traffic and competition increased, adding repair facilities and selling different brands of petrol during its working life. It is a small building, but it helps explain how an entirely new roadside economy developed around the automobile.

Pontiac

Pontiac has become one of the major Route 66 hubs in Illinois, with museums, murals and preserved roadside history concentrated around its downtown. It is tempting to arrive, photograph the large Route 66 mural and leave, but the town works better if you give it some time.

What makes Pontiac particularly interesting is the way preservation has become part of the town itself. Route 66 disappeared from the federal highway system in 1985, but communities like Pontiac decided that the road was still worth interpreting and celebrating. Museums, public art, historic buildings and local organisations now work together to keep that history visible.

That preservation movement is an important part of the modern Route 66 story. Without it, much more of the road would simply have disappeared.

Springfield

Illinois' state capital adds another layer to the journey because Route 66 runs through a city already deeply connected to American history through Abraham Lincoln.

The old highway passes through a landscape of diners, motels and former roadside businesses, while Lincoln's home, tomb and presidential sites tell a story that predates the automobile entirely. It is a useful reminder that Route 66 did not create the places it crossed. It connected communities that already had histories of their own.

Ariston Café, Litchfield

  • Food
  • Neon

The Ariston Café is one of the best places on the entire route for understanding just how much businesses depended on the highway.

Pete Adam first opened the Ariston in Carlinville in 1924, before Route 66 existed. When the new highway bypassed Carlinville, he followed the traffic and eventually opened the present restaurant in Litchfield in 1935.

Then the highway changed again. When Route 66 was widened and shifted behind the restaurant in 1940, Adam adapted by adding signage to the back of the building so motorists on the new alignment could still see it.

The Ariston survived because its owners kept responding to the road. That story would repeat itself thousands of times along Route 66 as alignments changed, bypasses appeared and businesses tried to remain visible to motorists.

Soulsby Service Station, Mount Olive

  • Gas Station
  • History

The Soulsby Service Station tells a similar story from the petrol pump.

Henry Soulsby had worked as a miner until an injury forced him to find another livelihood. Recognising the opportunities created by increasing automobile traffic, he built a filling station beside the road in the 1920s. When Route 66 was designated, that existing road became part of the new highway and the Soulsby family found themselves serving travellers from across the country.

The family continued operating the station for decades, but Interstate 55 eventually diverted through traffic away from the old road. Petrol sales ended in 1991, although the Soulsbys continued welcoming Route 66 visitors for several more years.

Today the restored station survives as a small museum. Its story spans almost the entire Route 66 cycle, from the opportunities created by the highway to the decline caused by the Interstate and, eventually, the preservation movement that followed.

Chain of Rocks Bridge

  • Original Route

At the Mississippi River, Route 66 once crossed one of the most distinctive bridges on the entire highway.

The Chain of Rocks Bridge opened in 1929 and became part of Route 66 in 1936. Instead of crossing the river in a perfectly straight line, the mile-long bridge takes an abrupt engineered turn near its centre, creating the unusual shape for which it became famous.

Route 66 traffic used the bridge for more than three decades before newer crossings made it obsolete. It eventually closed to vehicles, but today pedestrians and cyclists can cross it.

Walking onto the bridge is worth the extra time. After travelling across Illinois, crossing the Mississippi here makes the transition into Missouri feel much more significant than simply following the Interstate over another modern bridge.

Missouri

All states ↑

Missouri changes the character of the journey. The landscape becomes hillier as Route 66 moves through the Ozarks, and the roadside history includes motor courts, caves, old bridges and some wonderfully preserved examples of the businesses that once competed for highway traffic.

St Louis and the Mississippi

Route 66 followed several alignments through the St Louis area during its lifetime, which means there is no single street or bridge that represents the entire history of the road here.

That can initially seem confusing, but it is actually one of the most useful things to understand about Route 66. The highway was continually being modified as traffic increased. Roads were widened, bridges replaced and routes shifted in search of faster or safer journeys.

St Louis makes those layers particularly visible. Instead of looking for one definitive line, it is worth thinking of Route 66 as a network of roads that changed with the city around it.

Route 66 State Park and Times Beach

  • History
  • Nature
  • Original Route

Today's Route 66 State Park occupies land that once belonged to Times Beach, a small community that developed alongside the highway west of St Louis.

Its history took a dramatic turn in the early 1980s when dioxin contamination was discovered. Residents were permanently evacuated and the town was eventually demolished, leaving behind a landscape with a history very different from the usual neon-and-diners image of Route 66.

The park opened years later and preserves part of that story. Nearby, the old Meramec River Route 66 bridge survives as another reminder of the highway that once carried motorists directly through the area.

Times Beach is important precisely because its story does not fit neatly into Route 66 nostalgia. Communities along the road continued to change, struggle and sometimes disappear long after the highway's celebrated post-war years.

Meramec Caverns

  • Kitsch
  • Nature
  • History
  • Off-route

Meramec Caverns became one of America's most famous roadside attractions partly because it understood a fundamental challenge of highway tourism: first you had to convince motorists to stop.

The caves were promoted relentlessly through billboards, roadside signs, bumper stickers and painted barns, with advertisements appearing far beyond Missouri. By the time travellers actually reached the caverns, they may already have seen the name dozens of times.

That enormous advertising effort is almost as interesting as the attraction itself. Route 66 created a new audience of motorists travelling long distances, and businesses learned to compete for their attention long before they arrived.

Wagon Wheel Motel, Cuba

  • Motel
  • History
  • Neon

The Wagon Wheel opened in 1936 as the Wagon Wheel Cabins, when roadside accommodation was rapidly evolving from basic tourist camps into the motor courts that would eventually become the modern motel.

Its buildings were constructed from local Ozark sandstone, with rooms grouped into substantial stone structures rather than simple individual cabins. A 1939 AAA directory advertised private baths, enclosed garages and rooms costing between $2.50 and $3 for two people.

The motel still operates, which is what makes it particularly worthwhile. You are not simply looking at a preserved building or museum display. You can still spend the night in accommodation created for Route 66 travellers nearly ninety years ago.

Devil's Elbow

  • History
  • Original Route
  • Nature

The older alignment around Devil's Elbow follows the Big Piney River through one of Missouri's most atmospheric stretches of Route 66.

The road bends through the Ozark landscape in a way that modern highways rarely do, following the terrain rather than cutting straight through it. The river, hills and historic bridge make this section feel far removed from Interstate travel even when the two routes are not especially far apart.

Driving stretches like this helps explain why Route 66 is remembered so differently from the highways that replaced it. The slower road forces you to experience the landscape rather than simply cross it.

Boots Court, Carthage

  • Motel
  • Neon

The Boots Court opened in 1939 and quickly became one of the most distinctive motor courts on Route 66. Its Streamline Moderne architecture reflected the optimism of the automobile age, while covered carports beside the rooms allowed guests to park directly next to their accommodation.

Owner Arthur Boots promoted the motel's location as the "Crossroads of America" and advertised a thoroughly modern luxury: "Radio in Every Room." The first eight rooms cost $2.50 per night, and five more were added in 1946.

Decades later, the property came dangerously close to demolition before preservationists stepped in. It has since undergone extensive restoration and all 13 rooms are once again available to travellers.

Staying here is very different from staying in a modern hotel decorated with Route 66 memorabilia. You are sleeping in the real thing.

66 Drive-In, Carthage

  • History

Carthage is also home to a surviving Route 66 drive-in theatre, another product of the automobile culture that transformed twentieth-century America.

Drive-ins took the independence offered by the car and extended it into entertainment. Families and couples could arrive, watch a film and leave without ever stepping far from their vehicle.

The same cultural shift produced motels, drive-in restaurants and suburban shopping centres. Route 66 was not simply a road through that transformation. It was one of the places where it happened.

Kansas

All states ↑

Kansas gets only 12.8 miles of Route 66, but there is an extraordinary amount packed into them. The road passes through Galena, Riverton and Baxter Springs, communities shaped by mining and regional transport long before the first Route 66 shield appeared.

Galena and Cars on the Route

  • Gas Station
  • Kitsch
  • Shop

Route 66 enters Kansas through the former Tri-State lead and zinc mining district, and Galena's history reaches well beyond its modern roadside attractions.

The restored Kan-O-Tex filling station now known as Cars on the Route is famous for the old tow truck outside and its connection to the world surrounding Pixar's Cars. That makes it an obvious photo stop, but the filling station itself tells the more interesting story.

Places like this were essential infrastructure. Motorists needed fuel, oil, repairs and local information, particularly when cars were less reliable and long-distance travel required considerably more preparation than it does today.

The tow truck might be what gets people to stop. The station explains why there was something here to stop for in the first place.

Eisler Brothers Old Riverton Store

  • Shop
  • History
  • Food

The store known today as the Eisler Brothers Old Riverton Store opened in 1925 as Williams' Store, built and operated by Leo Williams one year before Route 66 was designated. The Eisler family came much later, purchasing the business in 1973. Longtime manager Scott Nelson bought it in 2011, so you may also see it signed and listed as Nelson's Old Riverton Store.

For local residents it was a grocery store and community business, while travellers used it for food, drinks, supplies and directions. When Route 66 arrived, the store did not suddenly become a tourist attraction. It simply found itself beside one of the country's most important highways.

That is a recurring Route 66 story. Many places we now travel thousands of miles to visit were once completely ordinary businesses serving everyday needs.

Rainbow Bridge

  • History
  • Original Route

The Brush Creek Bridge, usually called the Rainbow Bridge, was built in 1923 and is another Kansas landmark that predates Route 66.

Its distinctive concrete design is a Marsh Rainbow Arch, a bridge type patented by engineer James Barney Marsh. Several once carried motorists along the Kansas section of Route 66, but Brush Creek Bridge is the only one that survives.

It is a useful reminder that Route 66 was not constructed from scratch in 1926. The new federal highway stitched together existing roads, bridges and local routes into a cross-country system.

Baxter Springs Independent Oil & Gas Service Station

  • Gas Station
  • History

Baxter Springs' cottage-like filling station shows another way roadside architecture adapted to the automobile.

Early petrol stations could look dirty, mechanical and unfamiliar, so oil companies increasingly used architecture to make them appear respectable and welcoming. Some were designed almost like suburban houses, complete with pitched roofs and domestic proportions.

The Baxter Springs station later became associated with Phillips 66 and now serves Route 66 visitors. What looks like a charming little building today was once part of a much larger effort to make motorists comfortable with a new roadside industry.

Oklahoma

All states ↑

Oklahoma's relationship with Route 66 runs particularly deep. Tulsa businessman Cyrus Avery played an important role in the development of the federal highway system and in securing the route that became U.S. 66, while the state preserves everything from primitive early pavement to classic diners, folk art and neon.

Coleman Theatre, Miami

  • History
  • Neon
  • Original Route

The Coleman Theatre opened in 1929, only three years after Route 66 was designated.

Mining magnate George Coleman financed an elaborate theatre where local audiences and travellers could see films, vaudeville performances and live music. Its Mighty Wurlitzer organ remains one of the building's great features.

The theatre is a useful reminder that Route 66 communities were never simply collections of service stations and cafés. People lived full lives in these towns, and the highway passed through existing centres of entertainment, work and community life.

The nine-foot highway near Miami

  • Original Route

Just outside Miami survives something much less glamorous but arguably even more revealing: a section of road only nine feet wide.

Built before Route 66 existed, the road became part of the federal highway after 1926. Its width seems almost absurd today. Two vehicles could not comfortably pass each other while both remained on the pavement, which is why drivers sometimes had to move onto the shoulder.

The surface is now rough, patched and gravelly in places, so take it slowly, and leave it out if you are driving an RV or a low car.

Seeing it completely changes the mental picture of early Route 66. The highway did not begin as the broad ribbon of asphalt we imagine from later photographs. In places it inherited roads built to much older standards, and travelling across the country could be slow, rough and surprisingly intimate.

Ed Galloway's Totem Pole Park, Foyil

  • Kitsch

Beginning in 1937, retired teacher Ed Galloway spent years transforming his property near Foyil into an extraordinary environment of hand-built concrete sculptures.

The largest structure towers above the site and is covered in colourful reliefs and figures. Galloway continued working on the property for decades, creating something that was deeply personal rather than designed by a tourism company.

That individual eccentricity appears again and again along Route 66. Some of its most memorable attractions exist simply because somebody decided to build something unusual beside the road.

Blue Whale of Catoosa

  • Kitsch

The Blue Whale began in much the same spirit.

Hugh Davis built the enormous concrete whale beside a pond in the early 1970s, initially creating a place where his family could swim and play. It eventually became part of a larger recreational attraction and, decades later, one of Route 66's most recognisable landmarks.

Its appeal is difficult to separate from the fact that it was never supposed to become an international icon. It began as one man's slightly eccentric project for the people he loved and somehow ended up representing an entire era of American roadside culture.

Tulsa and Cyrus Avery

Few cities are as closely connected to the creation of Route 66 as Tulsa.

Cyrus Avery lived here and became one of the strongest advocates for a Chicago-to-Los Angeles highway running through the city. He is commonly called the Father of Route 66, although that nickname can make the process sound more individual than it really was. The federal highway system emerged through committees, negotiations and competing interests, and Avery's importance came from the role he played within that process and in promoting the resulting road.

The story becomes more tangible at the historic 11th Street Arkansas River Bridge. Completed in 1917, the bridge already provided an important river crossing before Route 66 existed. Avery had been involved with its construction while serving as a Tulsa County commissioner, and the existing crossing helped shape the future highway's route through the city.

Tulsa is therefore a good place to look beyond the mythology and think about the practical decisions that created Route 66 in the first place.

Rock Café, Stroud

  • Food
  • Neon
  • History

The Rock Café took several years to build before opening in 1939, and its history follows many of the larger changes that affected Route 66.

During World War II, Greyhound buses carrying military personnel helped bring customers through the door. After the war, traffic increased dramatically and the café moved to a 24-hour schedule. Its neon sign appeared in the late 1940s, just as Route 66 was entering the period we now tend to romanticise most.

The café survived the decline of the old highway and later a devastating fire, and it still serves travellers today. In one small building you can trace Depression-era construction, wartime movement, post-war prosperity, decline and modern preservation.

Arcadia Round Barn

  • History

The Round Barn was already nearly thirty years old when Route 66 arrived in Arcadia.

Farmer William Harrison Odor began building it in 1898. The sixty-foot-wide structure served agricultural purposes downstairs, while its upper level became a community gathering and dance space.

When Route 66 was designated through Arcadia in 1926, motorists suddenly began passing an unusual building that local residents had known for decades. Over time, what had been a community landmark became a roadside landmark as well.

It is another good example of Route 66 inheriting history rather than creating it.

POPS, Arcadia

  • Kitsch
  • Food
  • Gas Station

A few miles away, POPS proves that Route 66 culture did not stop being created when the highway was decommissioned.

Opened in 2007, the modern filling station and restaurant deliberately borrow from the visual language of classic roadside architecture. The enormous 66-foot soda bottle outside performs essentially the same job as the Gemini Giant or an oversized milk bottle did decades earlier: make sure drivers notice you.

The materials and architecture have changed, but the roadside instinct is exactly the same.

Threatt Filling Station, Luther

  • Gas Station

The Threatt Filling Station introduces a part of Route 66 history that is often missing from the nostalgic version of the road.

Allen Threatt Sr., a Black landowner whose family farmed and quarried sandstone in the area, built the sandstone filling station around 1915 along State Highway 7. When that road was later incorporated into Route 66, the Threatt family found themselves serving a growing stream of long-distance motorists. A grocery store was added in 1935 and a café followed in 1937.

During segregation, the station became particularly important to Black travellers, who could not assume that every filling station, restaurant or motel along their journey would serve them. The Threatt family welcomed Black motorists, providing fuel, food and even a place where travellers could park and sleep overnight.

The wider context is important. Beginning in 1936, Victor H. Green published The Negro Motorist Green Book to help Black travellers identify businesses where they could obtain accommodation, food and other services. The Threatt Filling Station itself should not be confused with a documented Green Book listing, but places like it formed part of the informal network of safe stops that Black travellers learned about through families, communities and word of mouth.

Route 66 is often remembered as a symbol of freedom and mobility, but that freedom was never experienced equally. The Threatt station is important because it allows both histories to exist in the same story.

The station is currently closed to the public and should be viewed from the road.

Milk Bottle Grocery, Oklahoma City

  • Kitsch

The Milk Bottle Grocery in Oklahoma City occupies only about 350 square feet, but it is difficult to miss because of the enormous milk bottle sitting on its roof.

The triangular building dates to 1930, while the oversized bottle was added later as advertising. It is a classic example of a simple roadside idea: if drivers only have a few seconds to notice your business, make the sign impossible to ignore.

The building's tiny footprint makes the effect even better. One of the smallest commercial buildings on Route 66 ended up with one of its most conspicuous advertisements.

Texas

All states ↑

Route 66 crosses roughly 180 miles of the Texas Panhandle, but the change in landscape makes the section feel significant. Towns become smaller, the horizon opens up and the road begins to look unmistakably western.

U-Drop Inn, Shamrock

  • Neon
  • Gas Station

The Tower Station and U-Drop Inn in Shamrock is one of the finest surviving examples of Route 66 roadside architecture.

The complex combined a filling station, café and commercial space beneath a dramatic Art Deco tower, giving motorists several reasons to stop in one highly visible building. Even the name was designed to attract attention: "U-Drop Inn" was submitted by a local schoolboy in a naming contest.

Today the restored building serves as a visitor centre and community space, but its architecture still performs the same basic job it did when the highway was busy. It makes you look.

McLean

McLean works best when you slow down enough to see the town rather than searching for one major attraction.

Its old filling stations, commercial buildings and signs retain the scale of a community built around a two-lane highway. Before Interstate 40, motorists did not pass McLean at seventy miles an hour from a road outside town. They came through its streets, where they might need petrol, food, repairs or somewhere to sleep.

That difference in traffic patterns explains much of what happened to Route 66 communities after the Interstate arrived.

Cadillac Ranch, Amarillo

  • Kitsch

Cadillac Ranch is one of the places almost everyone knows before reaching Route 66.

Created in 1974 by the experimental art collective Ant Farm and commissioned by Amarillo patron Stanley Marsh 3, the installation consists of ten Cadillacs buried nose-first in a Texas field. The cars represent successive generations of Cadillac design, particularly the rise and eventual decline of the tailfin.

The installation has not always stood exactly where you see it today. As Amarillo expanded towards the original site, the cars were moved west in 1997 so that Cadillac Ranch could retain the open-field setting that had become such an important part of the work.

Visitors began adding graffiti, creating the constantly changing layers of paint that now cover the cars. That participation has become inseparable from the work itself, which means the Cadillac Ranch you see today is quite literally different from the one somebody visited yesterday.

Historic Route 66 in Amarillo

Do not let Cadillac Ranch become your only Amarillo stop.

The city's old Route 66 commercial corridor tells another side of the story through surviving signs, shops, former filling stations and businesses that developed around everyday local traffic.

Cadillac Ranch gives you the iconic photograph. The old commercial strip helps explain the world that produced it.

Midpoint, Adrian

  • History
  • Kitsch
  • Original Route

Adrian's famous midpoint sign marks 1,139 miles to Chicago and 1,139 miles to Los Angeles, turning an otherwise tiny Panhandle community into a ceremonial stop for Route 66 travellers.

There is something satisfying about standing there and realising that the distance behind you is finally equal to the distance ahead.

But the town is also a reminder of what highway traffic could mean to small communities. Adrian mattered to travellers because it sat directly on the road, and travellers mattered to Adrian because every passing car represented a potential customer.

The sign celebrates geography. The town around it tells the economic story.

Glenrio

  • Ghost Town/Ruins
  • History
  • Original Route

At the Texas-New Mexico border, Glenrio shows what happened when that relationship between highway and town was broken.

During the Route 66 years, the tiny settlement offered fuel, food, accommodation and other services to travellers crossing the High Plains. It never needed a large permanent population because the road continually delivered customers to its businesses.

Interstate 40 bypassed Glenrio in 1975, allowing through traffic to cross the area without entering the town. Businesses that had spent decades depending on motorists slowing down, refuelling and eating suddenly found themselves beside a road most travellers no longer needed.

Today the historic district includes the old Route 66 roadbed and numerous surviving buildings. Seeing them together makes the consequences of the Interstate system much easier to understand than reading about bypasses in the abstract.

Just remember that abandoned does not mean unowned. View historic structures from public roads unless access is clearly permitted.

New Mexico

All states ↑

New Mexico contains some of the richest Route 66 history because there was never just one Route 66 here. The original highway travelled north through Santa Fe before a major 1937 realignment sent traffic more directly west through Albuquerque.

That means following Route 66 through New Mexico sometimes involves deciding which generation of the highway you want to follow.

Tucumcari

Few towns deliver the classic Route 66 visual experience as completely as Tucumcari.

Motels, murals, old commercial buildings and neon signs line the former highway, and this is one place where arriving before sunset and staying after dark genuinely changes the experience. Many of the signs were designed to be seen at night, when a glowing motel name could pull a tired motorist off the road.

Tucumcari is best understood as a streetscape rather than a collection of individual attractions. The concentration of surviving roadside architecture allows you to imagine what approaching a highway town after dark once looked like.

Blue Swallow Motel, Tucumcari

  • Motel
  • Neon
  • History

The Blue Swallow Motel was opened in 1942 by rancher Ted Jones after carpenter W.A. Huggins had begun construction before the United States entered World War II. Its L-shaped arrangement of rooms and garages was designed specifically around motorists, allowing guests to park their cars directly beside their accommodation.

Long-time owner Lillian Redman later became part of Route 66 folklore for the hospitality she extended to travellers. That human element matters because independent motels were not anonymous chains. The owner might live on the property, check you in, give you directions and remember you when you returned years later.

The Blue Swallow still operates, allowing travellers to experience a surviving motor court rather than simply photograph one.

Tee Pee Curios, Tucumcari

  • Kitsch
  • Shop
  • Neon

Tee Pee Curios began in the 1940s as a Gulf filling station that also sold groceries and souvenirs, but the building motorists recognise today is the result of later adaptation.

When Route 66 was widened, the petrol pumps were removed and the large stucco teepee entrance was added. Neon added another layer of roadside visibility.

The result is almost a textbook example of how Route 66 businesses changed with the highway. A practical filling station gradually became something designed to make passing motorists stop, look and buy a souvenir.

Santa Fe and the original alignment

If you follow only the later east-to-west Route 66 alignment through New Mexico, you miss an entire chapter of the road's history.

The original route travelled north from Santa Rosa towards Santa Fe before turning south again through Albuquerque and Los Lunas. In 1937, Route 66 was officially realigned onto a much more direct east-west route from Santa Rosa through Moriarty, Albuquerque and Laguna Pueblo.

It is one of the best examples of why looking for a single definitive Route 66 line on a modern map can be misleading. The highway was continually evolving as engineers looked for faster, straighter and more efficient routes. The older Santa Fe alignment is not a detour from Route 66 history. It is evidence of how Route 66 itself changed.

La Bajada

  • Original Route
  • Nature

The old road at La Bajada makes the difficulty of early automobile travel immediately understandable.

Southwest of Santa Fe, the historic route descended the escarpment through a remarkable series of tight switchbacks. Early vehicles had to negotiate steep grades and sharp turns that would be completely unacceptable on a modern highway.

Later improvements and realignments eventually left the switchback road behind, but traces remain in the landscape. It is one of those places where an abandoned alignment can explain Route 66 more effectively than a restored attraction.

This is not a section to treat as an ordinary scenic drive. Parts are extremely rough and portions cross Pueblo land where access requires permission. The point is not necessarily to drive it, but to understand what the old road reveals about travelling through New Mexico before modern highway engineering.

KiMo Theatre, Albuquerque

  • History
  • Neon

The KiMo Theatre opened in 1927, ten years before Route 66 was realigned along Central Avenue in front of it.

Its extraordinary architecture combines Art Deco geometry with Southwestern motifs in the style generally known as Pueblo Deco. The building survived fire, closure and the threat of demolition before being restored as a performance venue.

The chronology is what makes it particularly relevant to Route 66. The KiMo was not built because of the highway. The highway came to the KiMo when its alignment changed.

Albuquerque is full of these overlapping histories, where Route 66 became part of a city that was already developing around it.

Central Avenue, Albuquerque

Central Avenue is one of the best urban Route 66 drives because you can watch the architecture change as you move through the city.

Old motels, theatres, neon signs, restaurants and commercial strips reveal different periods of development, from the early highway years through the post-war boom and beyond. Some buildings are beautifully preserved, while others require a little imagination to recognise what they once were.

That mixture is part of the appeal. Route 66 was never frozen in its 1950s heyday, and Central Avenue shows what happens when a highway continues to function as part of a living city long after its federal designation disappears.

Laguna Pueblo

West of Albuquerque, Route 66 crosses the Pueblo of Laguna, which introduces another layer of history often simplified in traditional accounts of the road.

Route 66 did not cross an empty Southwestern landscape. It travelled through lands that had been home to Indigenous communities for centuries. As tourism increased, Pueblo communities existed alongside trading posts, curio businesses and roadside attractions selling motorists an increasingly commercialised image of the Southwest.

That relationship created economic opportunities, including markets for Indigenous artists and craftspeople, but it also encouraged stereotypes that treated diverse Native cultures as interchangeable pieces of roadside scenery.

The important thing when travelling through Pueblo lands today is to remember that these are living communities rather than historic attractions. Local rules around access and photography should always be respected.

El Rancho Hotel, Gallup

  • Motel
  • Neon

Built in 1936, El Rancho became closely associated with Hollywood as film crews used Gallup and the surrounding landscapes for Westerns.

Actors including John Wayne, Katharine Hepburn, Spencer Tracy, Kirk Douglas, Gregory Peck and Humphrey Bogart stayed at the hotel while working in the region, and that film history remains part of the building's identity today.

Even if you are not staying overnight, it is worth walking into the lobby. The huge fireplace, timber staircase and walls of Hollywood photographs immediately place you in a particular version of the American West, one shaped as much by cinema and imagination as by the landscape outside.

That relationship between the real West and the West sold to travellers is another recurring Route 66 theme.

Arizona

All states ↑

Arizona contains some of the most famous landscapes and roadside towns on Route 66, but it also played an important role in the road's final chapter. The last section of Route 66 in the country to be bypassed by the Interstate was at Williams in 1984, shortly before U.S. 66 disappeared from the federal highway system altogether.

Petrified Forest National Park

  • Nature
  • Original Route

Petrified Forest is not simply a national park near Route 66. Part of the historic highway actually crossed what is now the park, and traces of that alignment remain in the landscape. National Register documentation records a six-mile section of Route 66 within the expanded monument by the 1930s.

A line of old telephone poles helps mark where motorists once crossed the landscape, but the road is only one very recent layer of history here. The park contains archaeological evidence of thousands of years of human occupation, as well as a geological record stretching back hundreds of millions of years.

Few places put Route 66 into perspective quite so effectively. The highway that feels so historic elsewhere suddenly becomes a very brief episode in a much older landscape.

Painted Desert Inn

  • History

The Painted Desert Inn adds another layer to that story.

Herbert David Lore developed the original Stone Tree House in the 1920s, providing meals, drinks, accommodation and a trading post for motorists crossing this remote section of Arizona. The National Park Service acquired the property in 1936, and the Civilian Conservation Corps substantially reconstructed it before it reopened in 1940.

The Fred Harvey Company later operated the property, connecting it to the hospitality network that had already shaped western railroad travel before Route 66 became famous.

Today the Painted Desert Inn is a National Historic Landmark and visitor centre, preserving a place where national park tourism and automobile travel overlapped.

Wigwam Motel, Holbrook

  • Motel

Holbrook's Wigwam Village #6 is one of only three surviving Wigwam Villages in the United States and one of two on Route 66.

Despite the name, its concrete units are shaped like teepees rather than wigwams. That distinction matters because the motel belongs to a period when roadside businesses routinely borrowed Indigenous imagery with little concern for cultural accuracy.

The architecture is undeniably part of Route 66 history, but so is the way the American Southwest was packaged and marketed to tourists. Recognising both does not make the motel less interesting. It makes the history more complete.

La Posada, Winslow

  • History
  • Food

La Posada opened in 1930 as one of the great Fred Harvey railroad hotels and became one of architect Mary Colter's most ambitious projects.

Its story is particularly relevant on a Route 66 journey because it connects two different eras of western travel. Before motorists crossed the country on highways, railroads had already created routes, hotels, restaurants and an entire tourism industry across the Southwest.

As automobile travel expanded, grand railroad hotels declined. La Posada eventually closed and spent decades in railroad use before being restored and reopened as a hotel.

Today you can stay in a building that helps explain what western travel looked like just as the automobile was beginning to transform it.

Seligman

Seligman is now one of Route 66's most colourful stops, but its modern identity grew directly from the damage caused by being bypassed.

Interstate 40 opened around the town in 1978, removing the steady stream of motorists who had previously travelled through its business district. For shops, motels, cafés and service stations that depended on highway traffic, the effect was immediate.

Local business owners eventually began campaigning to preserve and promote the old road, contributing to the movement that established Historic Route 66 in Arizona. After the final Interstate bypass at Williams in 1984, Arizona became an important centre of the grassroots effort to preserve the old highway.

The souvenir shops, signs and deliberate nostalgia that fill Seligman today can feel theatrical, but they are also part of the town's survival story. Tourism did not simply commercialise Seligman's Route 66 history. It helped keep the town on the map after the Interstate had taken the traffic away.

Peach Springs

Peach Springs offers a different perspective on what the Interstate did to Route 66 communities.

Located on Hualapai land, the community had connections to the railroad and regional trade before Route 66 arrived. The Peach Springs Trading Post later served both local residents and highway travellers, with Hualapai people trading and selling crafts while motorists stopped for supplies and souvenirs.

When Interstate 40 bypassed the old road, the traffic disappeared. The bypass story is usually told through abandoned motels and ghost towns, but Peach Springs reminds us that the economic consequences also affected Native communities whose businesses had become tied to Route 66 traffic.

Hackberry

  • Shop
  • Kitsch
  • History

Hackberry's old general-store landscape delivers almost every visual ingredient people expect from western Route 66: weathered signs, petrol pumps, old cars and desert.

It is easy to enjoy it simply as a photograph, but isolated businesses like this existed for practical reasons. On long stretches of two-lane road, motorists needed reliable places to find fuel, water, repairs, food and local information.

The farther west you travel, the easier it becomes to understand how important those services once were. Breaking down in the desert or Black Mountains was a very different proposition from pulling into a modern Interstate service centre.

Kingman and Sitgreaves Pass

Kingman is a useful base for exploring western Arizona, but the real experience begins when you leave town on the old alignment towards Oatman.

The road climbs into the Black Mountains through Sitgreaves Pass, twisting through steep grades and sharp curves that would never be engineered into a modern Interstate. This is one of the stretches where you actually experience Route 66 as a road rather than moving from one attraction to another.

Early motorists had to contend with overheating engines, less effective brakes and fuel systems that could struggle on steep grades. Stories survive of drivers reversing uphill because gravity-fed fuel systems worked better with the vehicle facing the opposite direction.

Whether you are interested in cars or not, driving the pass makes the challenges of early cross-country travel immediately obvious.

Oatman

Oatman existed as a mining community before Route 66 turned it into a highway town, and the place has reinvented itself several times since.

Today the burros wandering through the streets, wooden storefronts and staged Old West atmosphere make it one of Arizona's most theatrical Route 66 stops. It is undeniably touristy, but dismissing it for that misses the more interesting story.

Mining brought people here. Route 66 brought another wave of traffic. When the highway moved, tourism became a way for the town to survive again.

The performance is part of the history now.

California

All states ↑

California gives Route 66 a final dramatic change of scenery. The road crosses the Mojave Desert before entering the Inland Empire and gradually becoming part of the enormous urban landscape of Los Angeles.

By the time you reach the Pacific, the empty desert highway can feel like another country.

Needles and El Garces

  • History

Crossing the Colorado River brings Route 66 into California at Needles, where El Garces immediately connects the highway to an older transportation network.

Opened in 1908, El Garces was built as a Santa Fe Railway depot, hotel and restaurant, with hospitality services operated by the Fred Harvey Company. Long before Route 66 carried motorists across the desert, rail passengers were already moving through Needles.

That overlap between railroad and highway is important. Route 66 frequently followed transportation corridors that had already been established because railroads had found practical ways through mountains, deserts and river valleys.

By the time the highway arrived, it was joining a landscape already shaped by long-distance travel.

Goffs

The older Route 66 alignment through Goffs is exactly the kind of place that disappears when the road is reduced to a collection of famous landmarks.

There is no giant statue or internationally recognisable neon sign here. Instead you find desert, railroad history, fragments of old road and the restored Goffs Schoolhouse, which is among the California Route 66 sites recognised on the National Register.

That relative emptiness is what makes it useful. Goffs gives you a sense of the distances motorists crossed between better-known highway towns and of the small communities that existed because railroads, mines and roads passed through otherwise isolated landscapes.

Roy's Motel & Café, Amboy

  • Neon
  • Gas Station
  • Motel

Few Route 66 scenes are as recognisable as the Roy's sign rising above the Mojave Desert, but Amboy is much more interesting once you understand why a business existed in such an isolated place in the first place.

Roy's began as a service station in 1938 and grew as Route 66 traffic increased, eventually offering motorists fuel, food, repairs and somewhere to sleep. In the middle of the Mojave, those services were not simply convenient. For travellers crossing the desert in older cars, they could be essential.

The town's fortunes changed dramatically when Interstate 40 opened through the area in the early 1970s. Motorists who had once passed directly through Amboy could suddenly cross the desert without entering the town at all, taking the customers that had sustained Roy's and the surrounding community with them.

That is what makes Roy's worth seeing beyond the photograph. Its famous sign belongs to the classic Route 66 landscape, but the near-empty town around it tells the other half of the story: what happened to roadside communities when the traffic they had been built around disappeared.

Barstow Harvey House

  • History

Barstow's Casa del Desierto is another place where the railroad and Route 66 histories meet.

The present Harvey House complex was constructed in the early twentieth century after an earlier building was destroyed by fire. When Route 66 was designated in 1926, the new highway passed through a town already established as an important railroad centre.

Today the restored Harvey House is one of California's recognised Route 66 historic sites and houses several cultural organisations, including the Route 66 Mother Road Museum.

Stand here and the evolution of long-distance travel becomes unusually easy to read. The railroad came first, Route 66 followed, and the Interstate eventually changed the pattern again.

Wigwam Motel, Rialto

  • Motel
  • Kitsch
  • Neon

The Wigwam Motel on the Rialto-San Bernardino border is the California counterpart to Holbrook's famous Wigwam Village.

Only three of the original seven Wigwam Villages survive in the United States, and two are on Route 66. Wigwam Village #7 is also recognised on the National Register as one of California's historic Route 66 properties.

Seeing both during the same journey gives you an unusual opportunity to compare two surviving examples of the same roadside concept hundreds of miles apart.

The California property also has its own preservation story. After years of decline, it was restored and returned to use as a functioning motel.

That is an important distinction. Preserving Route 66 does not always mean freezing a building in time. Sometimes the best way to save it is to make it useful again.

Foothill Boulevard and the Los Angeles suburbs

As Route 66 enters greater Los Angeles, the landscape becomes more difficult to read.

The desert disappears and the highway becomes an urban arterial surrounded by decades of later development. Historic theatres, motels, bridges and commercial buildings can sit between modern shops, housing and traffic, making the old road much less visually obvious than it was in Arizona or the Mojave.

That does not make this section less important. It tells the final part of Route 66's story as a working highway.

Route 66 did not end in romantic wilderness. It entered one of the largest urban regions in America and became part of the everyday street network. Following it here requires more attention, but spotting an old motel sign or surviving roadside building among the modern city can be just as rewarding as finding one alone in the desert.

Los Angeles and the changing western terminus

Route 66 did not always reach the Pacific.

When the highway was first designated, its western terminus was in downtown Los Angeles at 7th Street and Broadway. The route was later extended west through the city to Santa Monica, moving the symbolic end of the journey towards the ocean.

That changing endpoint is another useful reminder that Route 66 was never a single frozen line between Chicago and California. Its alignments changed, its cities grew around it and even its beginning and end moved over time.

Santa Monica

The End of the Trail sign on Santa Monica Pier has become the ceremonial finish line for modern Route 66 travellers, and after crossing eight states it is difficult to resist the photograph.

Historically, though, the pier was not the official terminus of U.S. 66. The later highway ended near Lincoln and Olympic boulevards in Santa Monica, while the pier sign is a modern symbolic endpoint created for travellers following the historic road.

That distinction does not make the ritual any less satisfying.

By the time you reach the pier, you have crossed major cities, farmland, the Ozarks, the Great Plains, Pueblo lands, mountain passes and desert. You have followed roads that were part of Route 66 for its entire life and others that carried the name for only a few years.

Take the photograph, then walk to the edge of the pier and look back east. The distance means considerably more once you understand some of what happened along it.

Why these Route 66 stops became famous

The great Route 66 icons work because they turn complicated history into something immediately visible.

A giant fibreglass figure tells you about the competition for motorists' attention. A filling station shows how an entirely new economy developed around the automobile. A motor court explains how accommodation changed when travellers wanted to park directly beside their room, while neon reveals what happened when businesses needed to remain visible after dark.

Even abandoned places tell a story. Glenrio and Amboy make the consequences of the Interstate system visible in a way that statistics never could, while an old bridge or strip of narrow pavement can reveal an alignment that has almost disappeared from the modern landscape.

But there are other stories here too. The Threatt Filling Station reminds us that the freedom associated with the American road trip was not available equally to Black motorists during segregation. Laguna Pueblo and Peach Springs place Route 66 within Indigenous landscapes whose histories began long before 1926. Hotels like La Posada and El Garces connect the highway to the railroads that preceded it.

That is what makes these places more than roadside props. Together, they tell us how Americans travelled, worked, advertised, migrated, built businesses and imagined the country during the twentieth century.

See the icons. Then look past them.

Before travelling Route 66, you will probably already know some of its landmarks. You will recognise Cadillac Ranch before you reach Amarillo, you will have seen photographs of the Blue Whale and the Gemini Giant, and you may already know exactly where you want to stand for your photograph at the Midpoint or Santa Monica Pier.

Go and see them. Being famous does not make them meaningless, and many became icons precisely because there is nowhere else quite like them.

But do not let somebody else's photographs decide your entire journey.

Some of the most interesting places on Route 66 are the tiny filling station in a town you had never heard of, the bridge built before Route 66 existed, the motel somebody saved from demolition or the old alignment that disappears into the landscape beside the road you are driving now. Sometimes the place you nearly pass without noticing carries more history than the attraction you drove there to see.

More than 250 Route 66 buildings, bridges, road segments and historic districts are listed on the National Register of Historic Places alone. Beyond those are countless diners, motels, signs, murals, former businesses, ghost towns, abandoned alignments and community landmarks that never became internationally famous.

That is why Route 66 rewards curiosity more than checklist travel. Stop for the giant, but look at the building behind it. Photograph the neon, then find out why it was installed. Visit the famous diner, but notice the abandoned filling station a few blocks away.

The icons give Route 66 its mythology. The places between them give it its history.
