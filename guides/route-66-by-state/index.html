Route 66 by State: Illinois to California Road Trip Guide

Eight states, one road, and a completely different mood every few hundred miles.

By Carole Richard12 min read

images/guides/route-66-by-state.jpg

The changing road

One of the best things about driving Route 66 is how often it changes underneath you. It begins among the high-rises and traffic of Chicago and ends beside the Pacific, but there is no single Route 66 landscape in between.

Over roughly 2,448 historic miles, the road crosses farmland, river country, Ozark hills, open plains, oil towns, cattle country, high desert, Native lands, old railroad communities, mountains and the Mojave. Even the road itself keeps changing. You will follow city streets, old concrete, frontage roads, forgotten loops and sections where the interstate has swallowed or bypassed what came before.

That is why it makes more sense to think of Route 66 as eight different chapters rather than one very long road trip. Each state has its own rhythm, its own history and its own version of what Route 66 means.

Mileage is approximate throughout this guide. The highway was realigned many times during its lifetime, so the distance you actually drive depends on which historic alignments you choose to follow.

Illinois

All states ↑

Illinois at a glance

Approx. Route 66 length

300 miles

480 km

Best for
First-time Route 66 travellers, small towns and classic roadside Americana
Best known for
Chicago, Gemini Giant, Pontiac, Atlanta, Springfield, Auburn Brick Road, Old Chain of Rocks Bridge
Did you know?
Illinois has several surviving giant fiberglass advertising figures, including the Gemini Giant and Atlanta's Bunyon Giant, making it one of the best places on Route 66 for classic roadside giants.

Illinois is the gentle introduction to Route 66, which is exactly what you want when you are still learning that a 300-mile day on this road is nothing like a 300-mile day on the interstate.

Leave Chicago and the city gradually gives way to old service stations, diners, brick roads, murals, roadside giants and towns that have made Route 66 part of their identity. Stops come frequently, so there is very little temptation to sit behind the wheel for hours at a time. You drive for a while, notice something strange beside the road, pull over, wander around and then do it all again twenty minutes later.

Places like Joliet, Pontiac, Atlanta and Springfield are also good introductions to the history behind the nostalgia. Pontiac has the Route 66 Association of Illinois Hall of Fame and Museum, while Atlanta preserves an original section of road built in 1924 that became part of Route 66 two years later.

Illinois teaches you how to travel Route 66 before the distances become bigger and the stops farther apart. Do not rush through it just because the landscapes ahead look more dramatic. This is where you learn to slow down.

Missouri

All states ↑

Missouri at a glance

Approx. Route 66 length

315 miles

510 km

Best for
Road history, Ozark scenery and old-school roadside attractions
Best known for
St. Louis, Meramec Caverns, Cuba, Devil's Elbow, Springfield, Gay Parita and Carthage
Did you know?
Springfield is closely associated with the birth of the Route 66 name. A telegram sent from Springfield in 1926 confirmed agreement on the number "66" for the new Chicago-to-Los Angeles highway.

Missouri makes the road feel older and a little less orderly. Once you cross the Mississippi, the landscape begins to roll, the road bends more often and the polished heritage-trail feeling of parts of Illinois gives way to caves, forests, rivers, stone buildings, motor courts and old alignments winding through the Ozarks.

St. Louis gives you the big-city version first, but some of Missouri's best moments arrive later. Cuba is packed with murals, Devil's Elbow still feels like an old highway settlement, and the road around the Ozarks gives you a much better idea of what early motorists were actually dealing with before modern highway engineering flattened everything out.

Then there is Springfield, a city with a particularly strong claim on Route 66 history because of its role in settling the highway's famous number.

Missouri is a state where taking the older alignment whenever you reasonably can pays off. The interstate will get you across it faster. That rather misses the point.

Kansas

All states ↑

Kansas at a glance

Approx. Route 66 length

13 miles

21 km

Best for
A concentrated dose of small-town Route 66
Best known for
Galena, Cars on the Route, Riverton, Nelson's Old Riverton Store and Rainbow Bridge
Did you know?
Kansas has by far the shortest stretch of Route 66 of any of the eight states, at only about 13 miles.

Kansas is over almost as soon as it begins, but that does not make it a throwaway state. Quite the opposite. Because its Route 66 mileage is so compact, you can actually take your time without worrying about a huge driving day waiting ahead.

The route passes through Galena, Riverton and Baxter Springs, with historic bridges, mining history, old businesses and plenty of small-town Route 66 character squeezed into those few miles. Nelson's Old Riverton Store has been serving travellers since 1925, meaning it was already there when Route 66 received its number.

Kansas is also a useful reminder that mileage is a terrible way to measure how interesting a section of this road will be. You could technically cross the entire state segment very quickly. Give it a few hours instead.

Oklahoma

All states ↑

Oklahoma at a glance

Approx. Route 66 length

400 miles

645 km

Best for
Route 66 history, old roadbed, museums and roadside oddities
Best known for
Coleman Theatre, Ribbon Road, Blue Whale of Catoosa, Tulsa, Arcadia Round Barn, POPS and the Oklahoma Route 66 Museum
Did you know?
Cyrus Avery, the Tulsa businessman and highway commissioner often called the "Father of Route 66," played a central role in creating and promoting the highway.

Oklahoma is where Route 66 starts to reveal just how much history sits underneath the neon signs and souvenir shields.

There is an enormous amount of old road to explore here, along with museums, diners, bridges, oil towns, roadside folk art and communities whose histories are inseparable from the highway. The landscape changes as you travel west too, moving from the greener northeast towards increasingly open plains.

You can see some wonderfully strange remnants of early motoring, including the narrow Ribbon Road near Miami, where the paved surface was only nine feet wide, as well as landmarks that existed before Route 66 itself. Arcadia's Round Barn, for example, dates to 1898.

Then there are the places that have become Route 66 shorthand: the Blue Whale in Catoosa, Tulsa's roadside architecture, the Round Barn and giant soda bottle at POPS in Arcadia, and the museums farther west.

Oklahoma is also where the road's story gets harder to reduce to simple nostalgia. Route 66 here is tied to migration, oil, commerce, Native history, displacement and the enormous changes brought by the automobile. Give Oklahoma time. It is one of the states that explains why Route 66 mattered in the first place.

Texas

All states ↑

Texas at a glance

Approx. Route 66 length

180 miles

290 km

Best for
Big skies, iconic photo stops and the classic American road-trip feeling
Best known for
U-Drop Inn, Amarillo, Cadillac Ranch, Big Texan and the Midpoint at Adrian
Did you know?
Adrian marks the traditional halfway point of the journey, with roughly 1,139 miles to Chicago and another 1,139 miles to Los Angeles on the commonly promoted Route 66 calculation.

Texas does not keep you for particularly long, but it knows how to make an entrance.

The Panhandle is big sky, wind, cattle country and long horizons, with Route 66 cutting an almost impossibly straight line through the landscape. After the towns and rolling terrain farther east, that openness can feel like someone has suddenly pulled the scenery apart.

The landmarks are suitably oversized. Shamrock's beautifully restored U-Drop Inn is one of the great pieces of roadside architecture on the route. Amarillo gives you the Big Texan and Cadillac Ranch, where a row of Cadillacs planted nose-first into the ground has become one of Route 66's most recognisable pieces of roadside art.

Then comes Adrian and the Midpoint. The painted line and sign are simple, but reaching them feels surprisingly significant. You have crossed half the road, and the landscape ahead is about to change again.

Texas is Route 66 at its most visually obvious, and that is part of the fun. Sometimes you do want the giant sign, the buried Cadillac and the photograph in the middle of nowhere.

New Mexico

All states ↑

New Mexico at a glance

Approx. Route 66 length

380 miles

610 km on the later alignment, with roughly another 100 miles if you follow the older Santa Fe loop

Best for
Neon, Southwestern architecture, historic alignments and taking your time
Best known for
Tucumcari, Blue Swallow Motel, Santa Rosa, Santa Fe alignment, Albuquerque, Grants and Gallup
Did you know?
Route 66 originally travelled through Santa Fe. In 1937, the highway was realigned on a more direct route between Santa Rosa and Albuquerque, leaving travellers today with two very different ways to cross part of New Mexico.

New Mexico changes the light.

After the Texas Panhandle, colours start appearing everywhere: adobe, turquoise, red earth, faded paint, neon, mountain silhouettes and a sky that seems to occupy half your field of vision. It is one of those places where stopping for a photograph because "the light looks good" can easily turn into half an hour beside the road.

Tucumcari is one of the great surviving neon towns of Route 66, while Albuquerque gives you a completely different experience as the old highway becomes Central Avenue and runs straight through a modern city. Farther west, Grants and Gallup bring mining, railroad and trading-post histories into the story.

But New Mexico also presents you with one of the biggest route choices of the entire journey. Before 1937, Route 66 swung north through Santa Fe before returning south towards Albuquerque. The later alignment cut more directly across the state. If you have the time, the older loop is not simply a detour for the sake of ticking off another alignment. It adds an entirely different historical and cultural layer to the trip.

This is not a state to rush. If your itinerary has room to breathe anywhere, let it breathe here.

Arizona

All states ↑

Arizona at a glance

Approx. Route 66 length

400 miles

645 km

Best for
Desert landscapes, historic towns and long stretches of old road
Best known for
Petrified Forest, Painted Desert, Winslow, Flagstaff, Williams, Seligman, Kingman and Oatman
Did you know?
Arizona is home to one of the longest continuous stretches of old Route 66 still driveable today, running for roughly 160 miles between Seligman and Topock.

Arizona is where Route 66 starts looking like the Route 66 people imagine before they have ever driven it.

The road crosses the Painted Desert and Petrified Forest before moving through high plateau country towards Winslow and Flagstaff. From there, Williams, Seligman and Kingman lead you progressively deeper into the classic western Route 66 landscape, eventually reaching the twisting mountain road towards Oatman.

There is a lot of mythology wrapped around this part of the route, but plenty of real history underneath it. Seligman became central to the modern Route 66 preservation movement after Interstate 40 bypassed the town, while communities across northern Arizona have built very different relationships with the old road.

This is also where many travellers make the detour to the Grand Canyon. It is absolutely worth doing if you have the time, but remember that the canyon is a side trip rather than a Route 66 stop. Give it its own time instead of squeezing it into an already overloaded day.

Arizona is a state where the landscape does a lot of the work. You do not need to fill every hour with attractions. Some of the best stretches are simply you, the old road and an enormous horizon.

California

All states ↑

California at a glance

Approx. Route 66 length

315 miles

505 km

Best for
Mojave Desert scenery, surviving roadside landmarks and the feeling of reaching the Pacific
Best known for
Needles, Amboy and Roy's Motel & Café, Barstow, San Bernardino, Los Angeles and Santa Monica
Did you know?
Santa Monica is the symbolic ending most travellers recognise today, but Route 66's official western terminus changed during the highway's lifetime and the road did not originally end at the pier itself.

California is a strange final chapter, which is exactly why it works.

At first, the road feels emptier than ever. You cross the Mojave through places such as Needles and Amboy, where surviving buildings can seem tiny against the desert around them. Roy's Motel & Café and its famous sign have become symbols of this section, but the abandoned businesses and fragile settlements around them tell just as much of the story.

Then the road starts pulling you back towards civilisation. Barstow, San Bernardino and the communities of greater Los Angeles gradually replace the open desert until Route 66 is no longer a lonely highway at all. It becomes city streets, traffic lights, suburbs and an enormous metropolitan area.

That contrast can catch people off guard. After thousands of miles spent chasing old motels, empty highways and small towns, the final approach to the Pacific is busy, sprawling and very modern.

Do not expect the last miles to feel like a perfectly preserved ending to an old American road movie. They are more interesting than that. Route 66 helped create a world of mass automobile travel, suburban expansion and roadside development, and then it delivers you straight into that world.

By the time you finally reach Santa Monica, you may be excited, relieved, exhausted or slightly unwilling to admit that the road is over. Probably some combination of all four.

Eight states, but never quite the same road

State lines make surprisingly good reset points on Route 66. Each time you cross one, pay attention to what changes. The road surface changes. The architecture changes. Food changes. Towns become farther apart or suddenly bunch together again. Mountains appear, trees disappear, the soil changes colour and even the light can make the road feel different.

That is part of what makes driving the whole route so different from visiting a collection of famous stops.

Illinois teaches you the rhythm. Missouri makes the road twist and deepen. Kansas concentrates it into thirteen miles. Oklahoma gives you some of its strongest history. Texas opens the horizon. New Mexico changes the colours. Arizona turns the landscape into something almost mythic. California takes that myth apart again before delivering you to the Pacific.

By the end, you probably will not remember Route 66 as one road at all. You will remember hundreds of little versions of it.
