## The stops between the stops

The famous Route 66 stops are famous for a reason. You should see the neon, the giant roadside figures, the old motels and the places you have been looking at in photographs since you first started planning the trip. Our guide to the [best Route 66 stops](/guides/best-route-66-stops/) covers them state by state.

But some of the best moments on Route 66 happen somewhere you barely knew existed.

They might be down an old alignment that looks like the wrong road, inside a building you would have walked past, or sitting quietly behind the attractions everyone else is photographing. Some are beautifully preserved pieces of Route 66 history. Others are a little strange, a little forgotten or simply still doing what they have always done.

That is what this list is about. These are not necessarily secret places, particularly if you have spent years studying the Mother Road. They are the stops that tend to disappear from shorter itineraries, or the ones whose story turns out to be much better than you expected.

### Auburn Brick Road, Auburn
::@ Original Route, History

One of the easiest ways to understand Route 66 is to stop looking for attractions and start paying attention to the road itself.

South of Springfield, a 1.4-mile loop on Snell and Curran Roads near Auburn follows the 1926–1930 alignment of Route 66. Shortly after the highway moved east, the road was widened and surfaced in hand-laid brick, and that surface survives today. It is narrow, quiet and nothing like the interstate running nearby. There is no big attraction waiting at the end. The appeal is simply driving it and realising what travelling through Illinois once felt like.

![A long straight stretch of red brick road with a yellow centre line running between flat fields under a stormy sky](/images/guides/auburn-brick-road.jpg "The brick road near Auburn, Illinois")

### Doc's Just Off 66, Girard
::@ Food, History

Doc's is the kind of place where the Route 66 story began long before Route 66 itself.

Deck's Drug Store opened here in 1884 and stayed in the Deck family for 117 years. The marble-top soda fountain, installed in 1929, survived, along with hardwood floors, pharmacy equipment, medicines and advertising collected over generations. New owners reopened the building as Doc's Just Off 66, keeping the pharmacy collection on display and bringing the soda fountain back to life.

It would be easy to treat it simply as somewhere to get a soda or ice cream, but take a look around while you are there. Much of what surrounds you belonged to the businesses and families who were serving this town while the automobile was still transforming American travel.

![A red brick storefront with a striped awning, a Docs sign and a Route 66 shield on a quiet main street](/images/guides/docs-girard.jpg "Doc's Just Off 66 on the square in Girard, Illinois")

### Turkey Tracks, near Nilwood
::@ Original Route, Kitsch

You could drive straight over this one and never know you had missed anything.

On an old section of Route 66 near Nilwood, what appear to be turkey tracks are preserved in the concrete. The story goes that birds wandered across the wet road surface while it was being laid, leaving their footprints behind before it hardened.

Route 66 history is not always a restored neon sign or a grand building. Sometimes it is a few marks in nearly century-old pavement.

### Ambler's Texaco, Dwight
::@ Gas Station, History

Built in 1933, this restored filling station served motorists for decades before becoming the Village of Dwight visitor centre. The pumps, canopy and service bays make it an easy place to understand what a small Route 66 station actually looked like before service plazas and interstate exits changed the way Americans travelled.

![A blue 1986 Chevy G20 van parked at the pumps of Ambler's Texaco in Dwight, Illinois](/images/about-road.jpg "Sullivan, our 1986 Chevy G20, at Ambler's Texaco in Dwight, Illinois")

### Bourbon Lodge, Bourbon
::@ History, Motel, Gas Station

Bourbon is said to be the only town in America named after the whiskey. On its western edge stands a much quieter survivor.

Alex and Edith Mortenson built the Bourbon Lodge around 1932 as a small highway stop in its own right: a stone café, a tiny Phillips 66 station and three tourist cabins. When the traffic moved to Interstate 44 the business closed, but the white clapboard lodge survived with its old sign still above the door.

It is a private home today, so enjoy it from the road and do not walk onto the property.

### Spencer, near Miller
::@ Gas Station, History, Original Route

Spencer is easy to miss, which is part of why it belongs here.

In 1925 Sidney Casey bought land here beside the old stagecoach road, betting that the coming federal highway would follow it. He was right. In 1926 he built a new store, and by 1928 he had added a row of buildings that served at various times as a café, feed store and barbershop, with a service station and garage at the end. When Route 66 was realigned in 1961, the traffic simply stopped coming.

The buildings have since been restored, but perhaps the most interesting part is outside: an old, unpaved section of Route 66 remains alongside the site. Instead of simply looking at an old business, you can see how the business and the road once fitted together.

![A stone café and garage with red vintage gas pumps and a Phillips 66 sign beside the old road](/images/guides/spencer-missouri.jpg "Spencer, Missouri")

### Gary's Gay Parita, Paris Springs
::@ Gas Station, Kitsch, History

Just down the road from Spencer, this recreation of a historic Sinclair station is packed with old pumps, signs, vehicles and Route 66 memorabilia. The original roadside business dates back to the early Route 66 era, while the station travellers see today owes much of its character to the late Gary Turner, whose hospitality helped turn it into a beloved stop for road-trippers from around the world.

![Gary's Gay Parita, a recreated Sinclair station with vintage pumps, signs, an old car and a green dinosaur](/images/guides/garys-gay-parita.jpg "Gary's Gay Parita, Missouri")

### Red Oak II, near Carthage
::@ History, Kitsch

Red Oak II is not an original Route 66 town, and that is what makes its story interesting.

Artist Lowell Davis grew up in the original Red Oak, Missouri, about 20 miles north of Carthage. When he returned years later and found his childhood community fading away, he began in 1987 to buy old buildings and move them to family land near Carthage. The result is a strange, affectionate recreation of an early twentieth-century town, with a general store, schoolhouse, church and 1920s Phillips 66 station.

It feels like a ghost town until you realise people actually live there. Visitors are welcome, but it is a private community, so stay on the gravel road and treat it as somebody's neighbourhood rather than a theme park.

![A shingled cottage with a green roof and blue trim reached by a narrow wooden footbridge](/images/guides/red-oak-ii.jpg "Red Oak II, near Carthage, Missouri")

Kansas has only 13 miles of Route 66, and its best-known stops, the Old Riverton Store, the Rainbow Bridge and the Baxter Springs station, are already in our [best stops guide](/guides/best-route-66-stops/#kansas). One more is worth adding.

### Galena Mining & Historical Museum, Galena
::@ History

Most travellers stop in Galena for the tow truck at Cars on the Route and keep going. Walk a little further and you reach the 1904 Missouri-Kansas-Texas railroad depot, now the town's museum.

Galena was founded in 1876 and named after the lead ore found in the surrounding hills. The museum tells that story with mineral specimens, mining equipment and a model of the nearby Grand Central Mine. It explains something the roadside attractions do not: why there was a prosperous town here for Route 66 to pass through in the first place.

### Seaba Station, Warwick
::@ Gas Station, History

Built in 1921, five years before Route 66 was designated, Seaba Station began as a rural filling station and later became an engine rebuilding shop. Today the restored building houses a motorcycle museum, but the real attraction is the building itself: one of the businesses that was already waiting beside the road when Route 66 arrived.

![The red brick Seaba Station with its open service bay, an American flag and a motorcycle museum sign](/images/guides/seaba-station.jpg "Seaba Station, Warwick, Oklahoma")

### Tank Farm Loop, west of Kellyville
::@ Original Route

The Tank Farm Loop is only around 1.6 miles long, but it gives you another glimpse of Route 66 before engineers began straightening and widening everything.

This narrow, curving stretch of 1926 paving carried Route 66 until 1938. There are no shoulders and little fanfare, just an old piece of highway bending through a former oil field that gave the loop its modern name. It is now listed on the National Register of Historic Places.

For most travellers it adds only a few minutes. For anyone interested in the road itself, those minutes are worth taking.

### Ozark Trail Obelisk, near Stroud
::@ History, Off-route

This might be one of the strangest pieces of pre-Route 66 road history left near the Mother Road.

About a mile and a half south of Route 66, down a country road outside Stroud, stands a 21-foot concrete obelisk. Before the federal numbered highways, the Ozark Trail was marked across several states with obelisks like this one. It originally stood on Stroud's Main Street and was later moved, and it is one of only seven that survive.

You find a weathered road marker standing almost alone in the landscape and suddenly realise Route 66 itself was layered over roads that already had histories of their own.

### Magnolia Station, Vega
::@ Gas Station, History

Colonel J.T. Owen built this small station in 1924 on the Ozark Trail, two years before the road became Route 66. Attendants sold fuel downstairs and lived upstairs, and over the years the building carried Magnolia, Phillips 66 and Conoco signs before ending its working life as a barbershop in 1965.

After I-40 bypassed Vega it sat empty for decades. A National Park Service Route 66 preservation grant helped the city restore it, and it reopened in 2004 as an interpretive stop. It takes ten minutes, and it tells the whole arc of a small Texas station in one building.

![Inside the restored station: a wooden desk and chair, shelves of vintage oil cans and green-framed windows](/images/guides/magnolia-station-vega.jpg "Inside the Magnolia Station, Vega, Texas")

### Lile Art Gallery, Amarillo
::@ Shop, Kitsch

Cadillac Ranch is not hidden. What happens to some of its paint afterwards is.

At his gallery on Amarillo's historic Sixth Avenue, artist and Route 66 historian Bob "Crocodile" Lile works with thick chunks of spray paint that fall from the repeatedly painted Cadillacs. He grinds and polishes the layers until they resemble brightly coloured stone, then sets them into jewellery he calls Cadilite.

It is funny, inventive and completely tied to a landmark everyone already knows. More importantly, it gives you something a photograph at Cadillac Ranch cannot: a chance to meet someone who has turned the road's ongoing culture into his own form of art.

### Glenrio to San Jon on the old alignment
::@ Original Route, Ghost Town/Ruins

Glenrio itself is in our [best stops](/guides/best-route-66-stops/) and [ghost towns](/guides/route-66-ghost-towns/) guides. What most travellers miss is the road west of it.

Instead of rejoining I-40, a 17-mile stretch of the pre-1952 alignment runs to San Jon through open ranch country and the remains of Endee. It was paved for many years until the county removed the surface, so today it is gravel and dirt. The old road follows the landscape at a slower scale, past traces of the railroad and earlier highway infrastructure.

**Check conditions before you go.** The road becomes impassable when wet, and after flash flooding washed out a bridge between Endee and San Jon in 2023, traffic has used a graded path down through the wash beside it. Drive it only in dry weather, in a vehicle you are happy to take on gravel, and skip it in a low car or an RV.

This is the kind of Route 66 driving that can look pointless on a map. Once you are on it, it often becomes the part you remember.

### Richardson Store, Montoya
::@ History, Ghost Town/Ruins

Montoya barely registers to most modern travellers, but the Richardson Store tells a surprisingly complete story about how transportation changed this part of New Mexico.

G.W. Richardson opened his store in 1908 to serve the railroad and the surrounding ranching community, and moved it into the red sandstone building you see today in 1918. When the highway arrived, the store adapted to motorists, selling groceries, auto supplies and other essentials to people passing through. Eventually I-40 took the traffic away again.

The building is not open to visitors, so this is a look-from-the-road stop. Still, it is an unusually intact reminder that Route 66 businesses often had lives before the road arrived and had to reinvent themselves when it left.

### Rio Puerco Bridge
::@ Original Route, History

West of Albuquerque, the old Rio Puerco Bridge gives you one of the most satisfying short walks on the route.

The steel Parker through-truss was completed in 1933 and once carried Route 66 across the Rio Puerco. Vehicles no longer use it, but you can walk onto the bridge and look along the old road as it curves away across the dry landscape.

There is something about standing in the middle of an abandoned highway bridge that makes the old road suddenly feel physical rather than nostalgic. This is not a recreation of Route 66. You are standing on it.

### Acoma Curio Shop, San Fidel
::@ History, Shop

The Acoma Curio Shop is easy to dismiss as another quiet roadside building until you know what happened inside it.

Lebanese immigrant Abdoo Fidel built it as a general store in 1916. From 1937 until 1941 he ran it as a curio shop, and unlike many tourist shops of the period, which mixed together replica souvenirs and work from many different places, he sold only work made at Acoma Pueblo.

The building is listed on the National Register of Historic Places, and its use has changed over the years, so check whether it is open before you plan to go inside. Its story adds something important to Route 66 history because it asks us to look beyond roadside architecture and think about what was being sold, who made it and how travellers encountered Native art along the road.

![A white false-front adobe shop with blue doors and window frames and a sun symbol on its facade](/images/guides/acoma-curio-shop.jpg "The Acoma Curio Shop, San Fidel, New Mexico")

### Querino Canyon Bridge, near Houck
::@ Original Route, Nature

About four miles southwest of Houck, Old Highway 66 crosses Querino Canyon on a steel bridge that feels far more remote than most Route 66 stops.

Built in 1929 as part of a major improvement and relocation of the highway across northern Arizona, the bridge still carries local traffic on Navajo Nation land. Below it, the canyon cuts through a rugged landscape that makes the engineering feel all the more improbable.

There is no gift shop or oversized sign announcing that you have arrived somewhere important. It is simply a piece of road infrastructure still doing its job almost a century later.

![A steel deck-truss bridge carrying the old road high across a rocky desert canyon](/images/guides/querino-canyon-bridge.jpg "Querino Canyon Bridge, near Houck, Arizona")

### Painted Desert Trading Post
::@ Ghost Town/Ruins, Original Route, Off-route

This is the closest thing on the list to finding a secret Route 66.

The Painted Desert Trading Post opened in the early 1940s beside the original highway west of Chambers, selling fuel, jewellery, rugs and supplies to passing motorists. A realignment left it stranded within a couple of decades, and the isolated building deteriorated until a group of Route 66 preservationists bought the property and stabilised it. Our [ghost towns guide](/guides/route-66-ghost-towns/) tells more of its story.

Getting there takes a little effort. The site is about a mile north of I-40, reached along unpaved Pinta Road, and the old highway beyond sits behind a locked gate on private property. Instructions for unlocking it are posted on the gate and on the Route 66 Painted Desert Trading Post Facebook page. Follow them exactly, close the gate behind you, and never simply climb over.

By the time you reach the building along the crumbling old pavement, modern Route 66 can feel very far away.

### Twin Arrows
::@ Kitsch, Ghost Town/Ruins

East of Flagstaff, two giant wooden arrows once stood in the parking lot of a trading post that opened in the late 1940s, pointing motorists towards its gift shop, gas station and diner. The business closed in the 1990s, and the buildings have been empty ever since.

![The abandoned buildings of Twin Arrows, Arizona, covered in graffiti, under a deep blue sky](/images/guides/twin-arrows-graffiti.jpg "Twin Arrows, Arizona")

Volunteers and the Hopi Tribe, which owns the buildings, restored the arrows in 2009, but one fell in 2022 and the site's future is uncertain. See it from the old road and respect the fencing. It is a sharp lesson in how quickly roadside advertising becomes a ruin once the traffic moves away.

### Old Trails Bridge, Topock
::@ History, Original Route

Near the California border, a huge steel arch rises above the Colorado River with no roadway left on top.

The Old Trails Bridge opened in 1916 for the National Old Trails Road, the transcontinental route that preceded Route 66 through much of the Southwest. Route 66 later used the crossing, and the bridge appears in the 1940 film of *The Grapes of Wrath*. When highway traffic moved to a neighbouring bridge, the deck was removed in 1948 so the arch could carry a gas pipeline, which it still does.

Seeing it helps put Route 66 into a much longer story. The Mother Road did not suddenly appear across an empty country in 1926. It inherited bridges, trails and roads that travellers had already been using for years.

### Bagdad Café, Newberry Springs
::@ Food, History

The real town of Bagdad, out in the Mojave, was bypassed and eventually erased. Its name survives somewhere else.

In 1987 the German director Percy Adlon filmed *Bagdad Café* at what was then the Sidewinder Café in Newberry Springs, a small 1950s roadside café on Route 66. The film found a devoted audience in Europe, and the owners eventually renamed the café after it. Today it is a slightly surreal stop: a modest desert café whose walls are covered in notes and flags left by visitors from around the world.

![The Bagdad Cafe sign in front of a shingle-roofed building covered in stickers, with white tables outside](/images/guides/bagdad-cafe.jpg "Bagdad Café, Newberry Springs, California")

### Elmer's Bottle Tree Ranch, Oro Grande
::@ Kitsch

Between Barstow and Victorville, more than 200 metal "trees" stand beside the road, every welded branch holding a glass bottle that rings and glows in the desert light.

![Hundreds of coloured glass bottles hung on metal trees at Elmer's Bottle Tree Ranch](/images/guides/bottle-tree-ranch.jpg "Elmer's Bottle Tree Ranch, Oro Grande, California")

Elmer Long built his first bottle tree in 2000, using bottles collected by his father, and kept adding to the ranch until his death in 2019. His family reopened it soon afterwards. Entry is free, so leave something in the donation box.

### Emma Jean's Holland Burger Cafe, Victorville
::@ Food

Some places survive because somebody restores them to look old. Emma Jean's barely needs the help.

The little roadside café has been feeding highway travellers and truckers since 1947, and its no-frills cinderblock building still feels like the kind of place you might have discovered by accident decades ago.

Go hungry. The Brian Burger is the signature order: a thick beef patty with melted white cheese and an Ortega green chile, between slices of Parmesan-crusted Texas toast grilled golden. But the real reason Emma Jean's belongs here is that it remains a working roadside café rather than a display about one.

![The empty counter and chrome stools of a small diner, the Open sign in the window](/images/guides/emma-jeans-holland-burger.jpg "Emma Jean's Holland Burger Cafe, Victorville, California")

## Sometimes the hidden gem is off the road

Not every hidden gem sits directly on Route 66.

Sometimes the best decision is to leave the road for a few hours. Route 66 was always a way of travelling through the country, not a rule that said you could never turn off it. If you have the time, places like Meteor Crater and the bigger natural and archaeological detours around the route are not interruptions to the trip. They are part of seeing the America the road passes through.

The trick is not to chase every possible side trip. Pick the ones that genuinely interest you, give them enough time, then come back to the Mother Road.

## Hidden does not mean untouched

Many of these places are [private property](/guides/route-66-travel-etiquette/), fragile ruins or small businesses trying to survive. Do not trespass for a photo. Do not climb on old structures. Do not treat abandoned towns like film sets. Spend money where you can, buy the soda, put something in the donation box. Preservation is not an abstract idea on Route 66; it is often the difference between a place surviving and disappearing.

When you share a hidden gem, add context rather than simply announcing a secret. Say whether it is private, whether a business is open, whether there is a gate to respect or a road to avoid after rain. Good travellers do not just discover places. They help protect them.

## The places between the icons

One of the easiest mistakes to make when planning Route 66 is building the trip entirely around the places you already recognise.

Those places matter. Go to them.

But leave some room for the old road that turns off beside the interstate. Stop at the shop you had never heard of. Walk across the bridge instead of photographing it through the windscreen. Spend twenty minutes in a town where your itinerary told you there was nothing to see.

Route 66 was never only a collection of attractions. It was thousands of miles of businesses, communities, landscapes and roads that existed between them.

A lot of the best parts are still there. You just have to slow down enough to notice them.
