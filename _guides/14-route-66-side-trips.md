## The side-trip question

Route 66 travellers face a constant temptation: should we leave the road? The answer is sometimes yes. The route passes near extraordinary places, and it would be a shame to ignore them simply because they are not technically on the alignment. But side trips can also consume time, energy and money. The trick is to choose the ones that deepen your journey rather than distract from it.

The detours below run from east to west, state by state. Each comes with a card showing where it is, where to leave the road, how long the drive takes and what it really costs your trip. Drive times are for today's roads in normal traffic, measured from the Route 66 town or exit named on the card. Opening hours and fees change, so check them before you set off.

### Bunker Hill

Bunker Hill is the smallest side trip in this guide, and one of the most rewarding for the effort. The town sits about thirteen miles south-west of Gillespie, off the original 1926 to 1930 alignment that ran through Carlinville, Gillespie and Staunton before the road moved east. In the middle of its main crossroads stands a bronze Abraham Lincoln by William Grandville Hastings, one of four Lincolns he sculpted. A former Union cavalry captain, Charles Clinton, gave the statue to the town in 1904 to honour the local men of his company. Some 7,000 people came to the unveiling. Lincoln looks down and points to a kneeling figure of Liberty, who is carving "With malice toward none" into the granite. It makes a good pair with Carlinville's Million Dollar Courthouse and Sears kit-home neighbourhood on the same early road. Together they show a side of Illinois Route 66 that most people speeding towards St. Louis never see.

::# Drive | 20 min · 13 miles each way
::# Time there | 20–30 min
::# Adds | About 1 hour
:: Where | Bunker Hill, Macoupin County, Illinois
:: Leave Route 66 at | Gillespie, on the 1926–1930 alignment
:: Getting there | IL-16 west, then IL-159 south into town
:: Good to know | The statue stands in the middle of the Washington and Fayette Street crossroads. Park on a side street. Free.

### Cahokia Mounds

Just before Route 66 crosses the Mississippi into St. Louis, it passes within a few miles of the largest pre-Columbian city north of Mexico. Around 1100, Cahokia was home to perhaps 10,000 to 20,000 people. What survives is a landscape of earthen mounds. The largest, Monks Mound, is a flat-topped earthwork about 100 feet high with a larger base than the Great Pyramid of Giza. A stairway climbs to the top, where the view takes in the mounds, the reconstructed Woodhenge sun calendar and the St. Louis skyline beyond. Cahokia is a UNESCO World Heritage Site and one of the most important archaeological places in North America. Most Route 66 travellers drive straight past it.

::# Drive | 20 min · 12 miles each way
::# Time there | 2–3 hours
::# Adds | Half a day
:: Where | Collinsville, Illinois, across the river from St. Louis
:: Leave Route 66 at | Mitchell, near the Chain of Rocks Bridge
:: Getting there | I-270 west, I-255 south, then Collinsville Road to the site
:: Good to know | The grounds and Monks Mound are open daily, dawn to dusk. The interpretive centre lobby is open Wednesday to Sunday, 9 am to 3 pm, while its exhibits are rebuilt. Wear proper shoes for the climb.

### Meramec Caverns

Meramec Caverns represents a different kind of side trip: classic roadside tourism. Lester Dill opened the cave to visitors in 1935 and promoted it relentlessly, with barn roofs painted across the Midwest and a Jesse James hideout story to go with it. Whether you love caves, signs or old-school promotion, it helps explain how attractions competed for motorists. The guided tour takes you through huge, lit chambers that stay cool all year, which makes it a welcome break on a hot Missouri afternoon.

::# Drive | 10 min · 3 miles each way
::# Time there | About 2 hours
::# Adds | 2–3 hours
:: Where | Near Stanton, Missouri
:: Leave Route 66 at | Stanton, I-44 Exit 230
:: Getting there | South on Highway W, following the signs
:: Good to know | Visits are by guided tour only, with tickets bought on site. Bring a light jacket: the cave stays around 60°F all year.

### Palo Duro Canyon

The Texas Panhandle looks like the flattest place on the route. Then, south of Amarillo and past the town of Canyon, the ground opens into Palo Duro Canyon. It is the second-largest canyon in the United States, roughly 120 miles long and up to 800 feet deep, with red and ochre walls cut into the high plains. The state park has a scenic drive down to the canyon floor and hiking trails, including the walk to the Lighthouse rock formation. On summer evenings the outdoor musical *TEXAS* plays in an amphitheatre built into the canyon wall, as it has since 1966. In Canyon itself, the Panhandle-Plains Historical Museum is worth an hour. Go early or late in summer: the canyon floor gets far hotter than the plains above, and the trails have little shade. Give it half a day, or a night in Amarillo, and Texas stops being the state you cross in an afternoon.

::# Drive | 35 min · 27 miles to the park gate
::# Time there | Half a day
::# Adds | Half a day, or a night
:: Where | East of Canyon, Texas
:: Leave Route 66 at | Amarillo
:: Getting there | I-27 south to Canyon, TX-217 east to the park, then Park Road 5 down into the canyon
:: Good to know | Texas state park entry fee per person. *TEXAS* runs on summer evenings only; check the season's dates. Carry plenty of water on the trails.

### Santa Fe is not a detour

Santa Fe belongs in this guide only because people keep calling it a detour. It is not one. In New Mexico, Route 66 gives you two roads, and Santa Fe is on one of them. From 1926 to 1937, the [original alignment](/history/) left Santa Rosa and swung north past Romeroville, through Pecos and over Glorieta Pass into Santa Fe. It then dropped south to Albuquerque and continued down the Rio Grande to Los Lunas before turning west again. In 1937 the Santa Rosa cut-off sliced straight west through Clines Corners and Moriarty to Albuquerque, and Santa Fe lost the highway. Both roads are Route 66. Choosing the northern loop means choosing the earlier road.

And it is arguably the better of the two. The 1937 line is quicker, but the loop carries more of the road's story per mile: the ruins of Pecos Pueblo and its mission church, the Santa Fe Trail's old approach, and a four-hundred-year-old plaza. On the plaza you'll find the Palace of the Governors and La Fonda, the Fred Harvey hotel that early Route 66 motorists slept in. The Spanish colonial capital the highway was built to reach is still there, and it is still walkable. Allow at least a night: Santa Fe is not a place to drive through. One warning: La Bajada, the notorious switchback descent south of the city, was bypassed in 1932 and has been closed by Cochiti Pueblo since 2017. Look at the escarpment from a distance; do not try to drive it.

::# The 1926 loop | 4 h 40 min · 240 miles, Santa Rosa to Correo
::# The 1937 cut-off | 2 h 50 min · 155 miles, Santa Rosa to Correo
::# Adds | 2 hours' driving · plus a night in Santa Fe
:: Where | North-central New Mexico, between Santa Rosa and Albuquerque
:: Leave the 1937 road at | Santa Rosa
:: Getting there | Today's roads follow the loop closely: US-84 north towards Romeroville, I-25 over Glorieta Pass to Santa Fe, I-25 south to Albuquerque and on to Los Lunas, then west to rejoin the later road near Correo
:: Good to know | Pecos National Historical Park is right on the loop. Book Santa Fe rooms early in summer and during Indian Market in August.

### Acoma Sky City

West of Albuquerque, a road leads south from the interstate into open mesa country and Acoma Pueblo. Sky City sits on top of a sandstone mesa about 370 feet high and has been lived in since around 1150, which makes it one of the oldest continuously inhabited communities in North America. Visitors ride a shuttle up from the cultural centre and walk the village with an Acoma guide, ending at the San Esteban del Rey mission, built in the 1600s with timber carried from distant mountains. It is a living community, not a museum. That is exactly why it is worth the trip.

::# Drive | 30 min · 15 miles each way
::# Time there | About 2 hours
::# Adds | Half a day
:: Where | Acoma Pueblo, between Grants and Albuquerque
:: Leave Route 66 at | I-40 Exit 102
:: Getting there | Follow the Sky City signs south to the Sky City Cultural Center, where tours begin
:: Good to know | Guided tours only, every hour from 9:30 am to 3:30 pm. The Pueblo closes to visitors for ceremonies, sometimes for several days, so call ahead (800-747-0181). Ask before you take any photograph.

### El Malpais

South of Grants, Route 66 country meets lava. El Malpais, "the badlands", is a field of black basalt flows, cinder cones and lava tubes, edged by sandstone cliffs. On the east side, NM-117 runs past the Sandstone Bluffs Overlook, with a view straight across the lava, and on to La Ventana, one of New Mexico's largest natural arches. It is a short, quiet drive that feels a long way from the interstate.

::# Drive | 35 min · 26 miles to La Ventana
::# Time there | 1–3 hours
::# Adds | Half a day
:: Where | South of Grants, New Mexico
:: Leave Route 66 at | Grants, I-40 Exit 89
:: Getting there | NM-117 south to the Sandstone Bluffs Overlook and La Ventana Arch
:: Good to know | Pick up maps at the visitor centre in Grants. Exploring the lava tubes needs a free caving permit. The lava is sharp: wear real shoes.

### El Morro

Further along the same country, NM-53 leads to El Morro, a sandstone headland with a pool of water at its foot. For centuries travellers stopped there to drink and left their mark on the rock: Ancestral Puebloan petroglyphs, then the names of Spanish colonists, beginning with Juan de Oñate in 1605, then American soldiers and settlers. More than 2,000 inscriptions survive. A trail climbs to the ruins of the Atsinna pueblo on top. It is the deep-time version of the Route 66 graffiti you see at old gas stations, and it pairs naturally with El Malpais.

::# Drive | 1 hour · 43 miles each way
::# Time there | 1½–2 hours
::# Adds | Half a day · a full day with El Malpais
:: Where | West of Grants, New Mexico
:: Leave Route 66 at | Grants
:: Getting there | NM-53 south-west, past the west side of El Malpais
:: Good to know | No entrance fee. The trails close earlier than the visitor centre, so arrive with time to spare.

### Petrified Forest and Painted Desert

[Petrified Forest National Park](/guides/route-66-hidden-gems/) and the Painted Desert are among the most rewarding landscape experiences connected directly to the Route 66 corridor. The park preserves a visible trace of the historic highway, including old roadbed and telephone poles. It also gives you geology, colour, silence and a reminder that the road's human history sits inside a much older natural history. The park road runs right through it, so this is a through route rather than an out-and-back: drive in from the interstate in the north and come out near Holbrook in the south.

::# Drive | 1 h 20 min · 47 miles, Exit 311 to Holbrook
::# Time there | 2–4 hours
::# Adds | Half a day
:: Where | Between the Painted Desert and Holbrook, Arizona
:: Leave Route 66 at | I-40 Exit 311, Painted Desert
:: Getting there | The 28-mile park road north to south, then US-180 west back to Holbrook
:: Good to know | National park entrance fee per vehicle. The road is gated at night, so check closing time. Taking petrified wood from the park is illegal.

![The pueblo-style Painted Desert Inn on the rim above the colourful Painted Desert badlands](/images/guides/painted-desert-inn.jpg "The Painted Desert Inn, on the rim of the Painted Desert in Petrified Forest National Park")

### Meteor Crater

Between Winslow and Flagstaff, a six-mile road runs south from I-40 to Meteor Crater. Around 50,000 years ago a nickel-iron meteorite hit the plateau and left a bowl roughly 3,900 feet across and about 560 feet deep. It is widely described as the best-preserved impact crater on Earth, and Apollo astronauts trained there before going to the Moon. The site is privately owned by the Barringer family and run as a ticketed attraction, with a visitor centre on the rim and guided rim walks. The detour is short and the sight is unlike anything else on the road. It also suits Route 66's taste for spectacle: a hole in the desert that the motoring age turned into a must-stop.

::# Drive | 10 min · 6 miles each way
::# Time there | About 1½ hours
::# Adds | About 2 hours
:: Where | Between Winslow and Flagstaff, Arizona
:: Leave Route 66 at | I-40 Exit 233
:: Getting there | Meteor Crater Road south to the visitor centre on the rim
:: Good to know | Privately owned and ticketed. The guided rim walk is the highlight. Check opening hours before you go.

![The rim and bowl of Meteor Crater in the Arizona desert, with a viewing platform on the far edge](/images/guides/meteor-crater.jpg "Meteor Crater, near Winslow, Arizona: a short detour off the road")

### Walnut Canyon

Just east of Flagstaff, Walnut Canyon hides cliff dwellings built by the Sinagua people some 800 years ago, tucked under limestone ledges in a forested canyon. The Island Trail loops around a promontory in the middle of the canyon and passes 25 of their rooms. It is one of the closest Indigenous sites to the road and one of the easiest to fit in: an hour or two, and you are back on I-40.

::# Drive | 10 min · 3 miles from the exit
::# Time there | 1–2 hours
::# Adds | 2–3 hours
:: Where | East of Flagstaff, Arizona
:: Leave Route 66 at | I-40 Exit 204, about 20 minutes from downtown Flagstaff
:: Getting there | Walnut Canyon Road south to the visitor centre
:: Good to know | The Island Trail is a one-mile loop with about 240 steps down and back up, at nearly 7,000 feet. It closes around 4 pm, and snow or ice can close it in winter. National monument entrance fee.

### Sunset Crater and Wupatki

North of Flagstaff, a single loop road links two national monuments. Sunset Crater is a cinder cone that erupted around 900 years ago, leaving black lava flows and ash fields that still look fresh. A little further on, the land drops into red-rock desert and Wupatki, where pueblos built of red sandstone stand against the horizon. The largest, Wupatki Pueblo, had around 100 rooms. The people who lived here farmed the ash the eruption had left behind. The drive tells that story in an afternoon.

::# Drive | 30 min · 18 miles to Sunset Crater
::# Time there | 2–4 hours
::# Adds | Half a day
:: Where | North of Flagstaff, Arizona
:: Leave Route 66 at | Flagstaff
:: Getting there | US-89 north to the 34-mile loop road, which runs through both monuments and rejoins US-89
:: Good to know | One entrance fee covers both monuments. You can walk around Sunset Crater but not climb it.

### Sedona and Oak Creek Canyon

Sedona has no Route 66 history to speak of. It is here for the landscape: red sandstone towers, buttes and canyons that are among the most photographed in the West. The approach is half the reason to go. AZ-89A leaves Flagstaff, drops down a run of switchbacks and follows Oak Creek through a narrow, wooded canyon before the red rocks open up. Come back via I-17 to make a loop. Be honest about the crowds: Sedona is busy, and the canyon road can be slow on weekends.

::# Drive | 1 hour · 30 miles each way
::# Time there | Half a day or more
::# Adds | Half a day to a full day
:: Where | Red Rock Country, south of Flagstaff, Arizona
:: Leave Route 66 at | Flagstaff
:: Getting there | AZ-89A south through Oak Creek Canyon; return via I-17 north
:: Good to know | Weekend and holiday traffic can double the drive. Many trailheads require a Red Rock Pass for parking.

### The Grand Canyon

The Grand Canyon is the obvious example. It is not Route 66, but it is close enough to the Arizona section that many travellers include it. If you have never seen it, go. But do not pretend it is a quick photo stop. The canyon deserves time, and the detour can [reshape your itinerary](/guides/route-66-itinerary/). Build it in properly or save it for another trip.

::# Drive | 1 h 20 min · 60 miles each way
::# Time there | A full day, at least
::# Adds | 1–2 days
:: Where | South Rim, Grand Canyon Village, Arizona
:: Leave Route 66 at | Williams
:: Getting there | AZ-64 north from Williams, then through Tusayan to the South Rim entrance
:: Good to know | National park entrance fee per vehicle. Summer brings long queues at the gate. Rooms on the rim sell out months ahead.

You can also leave the car in Williams and take the Grand Canyon Railway, which has run between Williams and the South Rim since 1901. It is slower than driving, but the train is part of the experience, and it drops you in the historic village by the rim.

::# Train | 2 h 15 min · each way
::# At the rim | About 3¾ hours
::# Adds | A full day
:: Where | Williams Depot to the Grand Canyon Depot, South Rim
:: Timetable | Leaves Williams 9:30 am, arrives 11:45 am. Leaves the canyon 3:30 pm, back in Williams 5:45 pm. In November and December, an hour earlier.
:: Good to know | Book ahead in summer. Stay a night in Williams to make the most of the day.

### Hoover Dam

From Kingman, US-93 runs north through desert to the Colorado River and Hoover Dam. The dam was built between 1931 and 1936, in the same years Route 66 was carrying Depression-era families west. It remains one of the great engineering works of the century. You can walk across the top, take a tour into the power plant, and look down from the Mike O'Callaghan–Pat Tillman Memorial Bridge. Strictly speaking it takes you into Nevada, a state Route 66 never touched.

::# Drive | 1 h 15 min · 75 miles each way
::# Time there | 2–3 hours
::# Adds | Most of a day
:: Where | Black Canyon, on the Arizona–Nevada line
:: Leave Route 66 at | Kingman
:: Getting there | US-93 north, then the Hoover Dam exit just before the bypass bridge
:: Good to know | Expect a vehicle security checkpoint. Parking is $10; the visitor centre is $15, the power plant tour $25 and the guided dam tour $40.

### Mojave and desert detours

In California, desert side trips can be beautiful but demanding. Heat, distance and fuel matter. Do not wander casually into remote areas without preparation. The desert is seductive in photographs and unforgiving in real life. If you are prepared, it can be one of the most memorable parts of the trip.

## How to decide

Ask three questions before taking a side trip. Does it connect to the story I am following? Do I have enough time to enjoy it properly? What will I sacrifice on the main route? If the answer is clear, go. If you are adding it because everyone else does, pause.

## Side trips that are really old alignments or older histories

Some of the best 'side trips' are not side trips in the usual sense. The Santa Fe loop is the clearest case, which is why it gets its own section above: taking it is not a detour from the story, it is choosing an earlier chapter. Bunker Hill works the same way on a smaller scale, a step off Illinois's first alignment.

![Evening on the main street of Las Vegas, New Mexico, with the El Rialto Restaurant sign lit](/images/guides/las-vegas-new-mexico.jpg "Las Vegas, New Mexico, near the original 1926 alignment to Santa Fe")

Petrified Forest National Park is another essential example because it contains traces of historic Route 66, including old roadbed and weathered telephone poles. The National Park Service describes it as the only National Park System unit containing a section of Historic Route 66. That makes it more than scenery. It is a rare place where deep geological time, Indigenous history, highway history and modern travel overlap.

## How to judge a side trip

Ask three questions. Does it reveal an earlier layer of the corridor? Does it help you understand the landscape the road passed through? Does it add meaning without stealing too much time from the main route? The Grand Canyon, Palo Duro, Meteor Crater, Cahokia, Acoma, nearby national parks, old mining towns and museum stops can be worth it if they fit your pace.

The danger with side trips is not that they are wrong. It is that they can turn Route 66 into a blur between famous destinations. Use them deliberately. A side trip should deepen the road, not replace it.

Side trips also help explain why Route 66 became a travel legend rather than only a highway. The corridor sits near desert parks, trading routes, rail history, Indigenous homelands, Spanish colonial towns, mining landscapes and Hollywood myths. The road borrowed energy from all of those layers.

## Protect the rhythm of the main trip

A side trip should earn its place in the itinerary. If it gives you landscape, history, rest or a better understanding of the region, it is probably worth it. If it only adds distance because a list told you to go, question it.

## Plan side trips around time, not FOMO

When adding major natural landmarks, avoid treating them as quick content stops. Places like the Grand Canyon or Petrified Forest deserve attention beyond a parking-lot photograph. Give them time or choose a smaller, more focused detour that suits the day.

## A detour should earn its place

Side trips can also help prevent road fatigue. After days of diners, signs and pavement, a national park, art town, cave, canyon or desert walk can reset your senses. The key is returning to Route 66 with more energy, not less.
