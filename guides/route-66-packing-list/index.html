Route 66 Packing List: What to Bring on the Mother Road

The best packing list is practical, not glamorous.

By Carole Richard5 min read

images/guides/route-66-packing-list.jpg
16:10 · approx 1600 × 1000 px

The packing philosophy

Route 66 packing is about comfort, safety and flexibility. You do not need to pack like you are crossing a wilderness for weeks, but you should not treat it like a city break either. You will move through cities, small towns, deserts, high elevations, windy plains, hot afternoons, cold mornings and long stretches where the next useful shop may not be close. Pack for the road you are actually driving, not the romantic road in your head.

Documents and navigation

Carry your licence, rental paperwork, insurance details, roadside assistance information and a backup payment method. International travellers should check licence and insurance requirements before departure. For navigation, use your phone, but do not rely on it alone. Have offline maps, a dedicated Route 66 guide or app, and ideally a printed overview. Phones fail, signals drop, batteries die and old alignments do not always behave the way modern navigation expects.

Car essentials

Keep water in the car every day, especially in the western states. Add snacks, a phone charger, power bank, sunglasses, sunscreen, hat, small first-aid kit, torch, tissues, wet wipes, reusable bags and a basic emergency kit. If you are driving your own car, check tyres, fluids, lights and spare equipment before leaving. If you are renting, inspect the vehicle carefully and understand how roadside support works.

Clothing

Pack layers. Route 66 can give you hot afternoons and cool mornings, especially at elevation. Comfortable shoes matter because you will walk more than you expect: museums, towns, murals, old streets, roadside stops, desert viewpoints. Bring something decent enough for a nice dinner, but do not overpack. Most of the road is casual. Dust, heat and long car days will humble any wardrobe.

Photography and memory

Bring the camera you will actually use. A heavy kit is useless if it stays in the boot. A phone can do a lot, but photographers may want a wide lens for landscapes, a longer lens for signs and details, and a tripod for neon. Bring extra storage and chargers. More importantly, bring a system for recording names and locations. After three weeks, every gas station photo starts to blur unless you log it.

Health and comfort

Long road trips are tiring. Bring any medication you need, plus basic pain relief, allergy tablets if relevant, lip balm, moisturiser, and enough water to avoid dehydration. Stretch at stops. Do not underestimate driving fatigue. Route 66 is slower than interstate travel, which is wonderful, but it also requires attention. Old road surfaces, small-town traffic, unfamiliar signs and photo stops all use energy.

What to leave behind

Leave behind the fantasy that you need a perfect outfit for every stop, a rigid schedule for every hour or a suitcase full of just-in-case items. Leave behind the idea that souvenirs are the only proof of travel. Leave room in the car, the itinerary and the day. Route 66 is generous, but it rewards travellers who are light enough to stop.

Packing for an old road in a modern country

Route 66 is not wilderness in the pure sense, but parts of it ask for old-fashioned preparedness. The road crosses large cities, small towns, farming country, mountains, high desert and the Mojave. Weather can shift from humid Midwest storms to dry desert heat, cold nights and winter snow in higher elevations. The original highway was a practical road; modern travellers should treat it with the same practicality.

A printed map is not nostalgia. It is backup. A dedicated Route 66 guide or app helps with alignments, but a paper overview helps you understand where you are when phone signal drops or a detour appears. Carry water, especially in the western states. Carry sun protection, a basic first-aid kit, a tyre-pressure gauge if you are driving your own vehicle, chargers, a torch and a small notebook.

Historical packing thought

One reason Route 66 became famous is that it made independent travel feel possible. But independent travel has always required self-reliance. Dust Bowl migrants packed what they could carry. Post-war families packed cars for long summer drives. Motorcyclists pack with ruthless discipline. Your packing list is a modern version of the same question: what do I need to keep moving safely while leaving enough room for surprise?

Leave behind anything that makes you rush. Too many outfits, too much camera gear, too many fixed bookings and too many imagined obligations can make the trip feel like work. Pack the essentials, then protect your attention.

Another practical fact: many classic stops keep local hours rather than big-attraction hours. A museum might be closed on a weekday, a cafe might shut early, and a family-run shop may open around the owner's life rather than a corporate schedule. Packing patience, snacks and backup plans is as important as packing clothes.

Pack for long days, not just long miles

The best packed item is often not an object but a habit: reset the car every morning. Put water within reach, check chargers, clear rubbish, confirm the first stops and make sure the camera or phone is ready. A tidy car makes a long trip feel calmer.

Keep the car organised

Pack for collecting, even if you are not a souvenir person. Route 66 produces small paper memories: postcards, museum brochures, motel key cards, diner receipts, maps, stickers and tickets. A simple folder or envelope keeps those pieces from becoming a crushed mess in the glovebox.

Leave room for what you collect

Leave physical room for the road. You may buy books, shirts, enamel pins, vintage objects, local food or unexpected finds. An overstuffed car on day one becomes a problem by day ten. Space is a practical luxury.
